Enterprise Zero Trust Architecture

A pre-sales solution design for a multi-site hospitality enterprise — 2,200 employees, three data centers, fifteen properties — running a fragmented legacy firewall estate over MPLS and IPSec. The brief: diagnose why the network was failing the business, then propose an architecture that fixes it without a rip-and-replace.

Zero Trust / ZTNA SASE Next-Gen Firewall SD-WAN MPLS / IPSec Okta / IAM Segmentation

The problem

The network had grown organically. Each site ran different hardware from a different vendor on a different update cycle, and every packet — including SaaS traffic — was hairpinned back through a data center before reaching the internet.

The hard part

Not the technology — the translation. Every recommendation had to land as a business outcome for a non-technical audience, and the answer couldn't be "replace everything at once." Budget and change fatigue are real constraints.

The approach

Map each business pain point to exactly one capability, consolidate onto a single policy plane, and sequence the rollout so the most visible pain is fixed first and pays for the next phase.

DISCOVERY

What was actually on the network

EDGE & CORE

Three vendors, one network

One vendor's switching and routing formed the backbone, a second vendor's firewalls and VPN concentrators sat in the data centers, and a third vendor's firewalls guarded the properties. Three consoles, three rule syntaxes, three patch cadences — and no single place to answer "what is our policy?"

TRANSPORT

MPLS with IPSec overlay

Sites reached each other over expensive MPLS circuits with IPSec tunnels layered on top. The design predated the company's move to SaaS, so all cloud-bound traffic took a detour through a data center it had no reason to visit.

IDENTITY

Okta — the one asset worth keeping

A mature IdP was already in place. Most of the estate needed replacing; this did not. It became the anchor for the entire Zero Trust design, since identity-driven access is only as good as the identity source behind it.

BLIND SPOT

No visibility past the tunnel

When a remote employee said "it's slow," nobody could say whether the problem was their home Wi-Fi, their ISP, the VPN concentrator, or the application. Every ticket started with guessing.

The analogy I used to explain SaaS backhaul to the non-technical stakeholders in the room:

"Forcing SaaS traffic through the data center is like ordering delivery, but the driver has to stop at corporate headquarters first so someone can approve it — and then bring it to you. By the time your food arrives, it's cold. That's what's happening to your applications."

This is the part of the work I care most about. An architecture nobody in the room understands doesn't get funded.

ALIGNMENT

One capability per problem

The discipline here was refusing to throw a product catalog at the customer. Every line below exists because a specific business pain justified it — and anything that didn't map to a stated pain didn't make the deck.

ACCESS LAYER

ZTNA replaces the VPN tunnel

Instead of dropping an authenticated user onto the network and trusting them, access is granted per application, with identity and device posture verified continuously. Lateral movement — the thing that turns one compromised laptop into a ransomware event — stops being free.

TRANSPORT

SD-WAN over commodity links

Application-aware path selection over broadband alongside the existing circuits, so MPLS spend can taper as confidence grows rather than being cut on day one. This was the line item that made the finance case work.

POLICY PLANE

Define once, push everywhere

Centralized management collapses three consoles into one. The operational win isn't fewer clicks — it's that misconfiguration, the most common root cause of a breach in an estate like this, gets structurally harder.

OBSERVABILITY

End-to-end experience monitoring

Synthetic and real-user telemetry across every hop from the endpoint's Wi-Fi to the application. "It's slow" becomes a diagnosis instead of a debate, which is worth more to a small IT team than most security features.

SEQUENCING

Why not all at once

The obvious recommendation is to replace the whole estate. The obvious recommendation is also the one that doesn't get approved. So the proposal led with the access layer:

PHASE 1

Cloud-delivered access first. It fixes the three most visible complaints — slow SaaS, painful VPN, no remote visibility — without touching a single piece of on-premises hardware. Fastest path to a user base that notices the change.

PHASE 2

Branch transport modernization, letting MPLS spend taper naturally as traffic shifts. The savings here help fund what follows.

PHASE 3

Firewall consolidation at the data centers and properties, aligned to existing hardware refresh cycles rather than fighting them.

I also prepared for the objection I knew was coming — "is this a hard transition?" — and answered it honestly: yes, there's a real learning curve, but the team ends up learning one platform instead of maintaining fluency in three. Pretending a migration is painless is how you lose credibility in the room.

REFLECTION

What I'd do differently

The framework came too late

I arrived at a clean structure — business problem → impact → technical solution → future state — only near the end, and then had to retrofit the deck to it. Starting from that spine would have made the whole thing sharper and taken half the time. It's the format I've used for every technical proposal since.

Research beyond the brief

Hospitality networking was new to me. I closed the gap by diagramming the traffic flows myself, reading vendor documentation on cloud-delivered security, studying a published enterprise reference in the same industry, and speaking directly with two engineers working in the field. Asking people who do the job is underrated.

The thing this exercise taught me that no amount of coding had: a technically correct answer that the room doesn't follow is worth nothing. The hardest and most valuable part of the work was compressing an architecture into language a finance stakeholder and a network engineer could both act on — and being honest about the trade-offs instead of selling past them.
